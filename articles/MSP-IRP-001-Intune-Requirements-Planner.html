There’s nothing to install and nothing to sign in to — just have these to hand. Don’t worry about being precise; “about 40” and “not sure” are perfectly good answers.

Step One
Answer the Scope Questions

The planner opens with six short questions — who you are, which kinds of device you have, how they’re used, what you’re licensed for, and whether Cyber Essentials is part of this project.

We use those answers to lay out a realistic starting point rather than handing you a blank page. If you have staff laptops, a shared tablet trolley and people using their own phones, you’ll get three device groups already sketched out, with sensible settings on each. You then correct them.

The question that matters most is “how are the devices used?” A laptop belonging to one person and a tablet a whole class shares need completely different setup — different enrolment, different security rules, sometimes different licensing. That’s why they become separate groups from the start.

You can reopen these questions any time with the Scope questions button. Doing so rebuilds the device groups, but keeps your people, apps and Wi-Fi list.

Step Two
Work Through Each Device Group

A device group is a set of devices that should all behave the same way. Click details on one to open it up.

Inside you’ll find everything about that set of devices: roughly how many there are, who uses them, how they get set up out of the box, the health rules they must meet, the settings we push out, which apps go on them, and how updates are handled. Every section explains itself, and the fields you don’t recognise can be left alone — we fill in the gaps.

Split a group whenever the answer to “who uses this and how” changes. Staff and pupils, one-per-person and shared, devices you own and devices they own — those all want to be separate. Use copy to duplicate a group you’ve already set up and then change what’s different.

A group can have more than one of anything. If half your laptops arrive pre-registered from the supplier and half are already in a cupboard, add two enrolment methods and tell us which is which in the notes.

Step Three
List Your Apps, Wi-Fi and People

Underneath the device groups are three shared lists. Fill each one in once, then tick things onto the groups that need them.

Apps — the software people actually need. Don’t worry about how it gets installed; that’s our problem. Just tell us what it is and which kinds of device need it.

Wi-Fi, VPN and certificates — anything a device needs before someone can use it. This matters most on shared and single-purpose devices: nobody is standing there to type a Wi-Fi password, so the network has to be pushed to the device automatically.

People and user groups — who each set of devices is for. You don’t need to list every pupil; describe the group in your own words and we’ll build it from your directory. Do name whoever will administer Intune day to day, and whoever should get the first test device.

Settings only reach devices through a group, so this section is what makes the rest of the plan real.

Step Four
Read What the Checker Tells You

The panel on the right updates as you work. It isn’t a form validator — it’s there to catch the handful of things that genuinely bite later, and it explains why each one matters rather than just flagging it.

A red item is worth fixing or discussing before we quote. An amber one is worth a conversation. Blue ones are just things worth knowing. Green means that part looks right.

Some of what it says will be about what you don’t need. If Cyber Essentials is part of this project, the checker will also tell you where the scheme asks for less than people commonly assume — that’s deliberate, and it saves money.

Use show me on any item to jump straight to the thing it’s talking about. And don’t feel you have to clear the list — some findings are decisions for us to make together, not mistakes for you to fix.

Worth Knowing
Why It Asks What It Asks
Guardrails, Not Hurdles

Every question here exists because skipping it has cost somebody time

The planner has opinions, and they follow published Microsoft guidance and well-established community baselines rather than house style. These are the things that go wrong on device rollouts, and finding them now costs a conversation instead of a re-run.

What It Steers You Towards
Decisions that hold up
  • Separating shared devices from one-per-person devices, because they need genuinely different setup
  • Naming who each set of devices is for — nothing reaches a device except through a group of people
  • Being honest about personally-owned devices, so we protect the work data rather than pretending we manage the phone
  • A short list of apps that must land before first use, and everything else afterwards
What It Saves You From
The classic surprises
  • Discovering at go-live that the licence you bought never included device management — some Microsoft licence names are only one word apart
  • Security rules that look green in the portal and are checking nothing, because they were pointed at the wrong thing
  • Sign-in methods that work beautifully on a personal laptop and are meaningless on a shared one
  • Tablets bought with app licences tied to people, for devices that have no person attached to them
  • Devices that restart with no warning, which is how a good rollout gets a bad reputation in week one

If You Get Stuck
Common Questions

Questions & Answers

I don’t know the answer to something — what do I put?

Leave it blank, or type what you do know into the notes box. “Not sure” is genuinely useful information — it tells us where to spend time. The planner never requires a field to be filled in.

What does “shared between people” actually mean?

That no single person owns the device. A trolley of tablets a class works through, a laptop kept in a meeting room, a hot desk. If a device goes home with the same person every night, it’s one-per-person even if somebody else occasionally borrows it.

It matters because a shared device can’t use sign-in methods that are set up per person, and it usually shouldn’t have anybody’s files on it.

We have staff using their own phones. Do those count?

Yes — add them as a device group with personally owned ticked. It doesn’t mean we take over anybody’s phone; in almost every case we protect the work data inside the work apps and never touch the rest of the device. The planner will steer you there.

The checker says something is wrong but I think it’s right

Then it probably is, and the checker is missing context. Leave it flagged and mention it in the notes — it becomes an agenda item rather than a problem. The checker is there to start conversations, not to grade your answers.

Can I come back to this later?

Yes. Your draft saves automatically in this browser as you work, so you can close the page and pick it up later — on the same computer, in the same browser. If you want to move it to another machine, use Copy share code and paste that code into Load a plan on the other one.

Can somebody else fill in part of it?

Yes, though not at the same time. Send them your share code; they paste it into Load a plan, add their part, and send a new code back. It’s a hand-off rather than live collaboration.

What are the “For your Active8 engineer” buttons?

They turn your answers into the technical documents we work from — a build checklist, a list of things worth discussing, and a machine-readable copy. You’re very welcome to read them, but they’re written for an engineer, so expect jargon. You don’t need to send them; the Copy as text output contains everything.

Is anything I type here sent to you automatically?

No. The planner makes no internet connections whatsoever — there is nothing for it to send anything to. Your draft is stored by your own browser on your own computer. We see it when, and only when, you email it to us.
